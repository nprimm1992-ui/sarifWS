A deliverable is a graph. The nodes are the claims. The edges are the reasoning that connects them — this recommendation exists because of this evidence, this line item assumes this uptake rate, this risk is mitigated by this mechanism. The graph is what makes the deliverable a system rather than a list of assertions.

Coherence Decay is what happens to the edges.

## The order of decay

Detail is not the first casualty. That is the common assumption and it is wrong. A re-narrated document usually keeps its numbers, its bullet points, its topline recommendations. What it loses — silently, triggering no review flag — is the connective tissue between them.

Three edges die first:

**Intervention to evidence.** A recommendation that named its comparable case, its measured effect and its follow-up window becomes a recommendation that says research supports it. What remains cannot be challenged at the source, because the source is no longer named.

**Line item to assumption.** A figure that carried its uptake rate, its horizon and the comparable it was calibrated against becomes a figure. The number survives; the assumption that determines whether it is credible does not.

**Risk to mitigation.** A contingency that named its trigger and its revised envelope becomes silence. Not a weaker statement. Absence.

In all three the *node* survives and the *edge* dies.

## This is measured, not asserted

Coherence Decay sounds like a metaphor. It has been quantified, in the most controlled handoff environment there is.

Carter, Davis, Evans and Cone studied trauma handovers at a Level I trauma center and published in *Prehospital Emergency Care* in 2009: ninety-six handovers, video-recorded, audited element by element against what the receiving team documented. Face-to-face verbal contact, one patient at a time, trained clinicians on both ends — no page budget, no reading-time expectation, no slide count.

Of 473 clinical data elements transmitted, 329 were received. **69.6 percent.**

<Stat value="30.4%" label="Of clinical information lost in a single optimal handoff — Carter et al., Prehospital Emergency Care, 2009" />

The selectivity is the finding. Mechanism of injury was transmitted 94 times and survived; anatomic location 81, age 67. But prehospital hypotension was received in 10 of the 28 times it was transmitted, Glasgow Coma Scale score 10 of 22, pulse rate **13 of 49**. What survived identifies the patient. What died determines the treatment.

<Callout kind="note" title="Why this study and not our own">
A firm measuring its own fidelity loss is grading its own work. This is peer-reviewed, independently citable, and describes a handoff better resourced than any strategic engagement — the optimistic bound.
</Callout>

Now compound it. Analysis to summary to brief to presentation is four handoffs, and at that retention rate four layers leave **23.4 percent** of the original signal standing. Two — the minimum any distributed team runs — already lose more than half. That is the arithmetic every organization runs without measuring.

## Where the compression happens

Every engagement has a seam: the moment the analytic layer hands its work to the apparatus that will deploy it. The compression there is not malicious or negligent — it is architectural. The one-pager has a page budget, the memo a reading-time expectation, the deck a slide count. Every format constraint that optimizes for the reader's time optimizes against the reasoning that justifies what they are reading.

Engagement 001 produced a leaders' brief whose stated purpose was the decision case compressed for a council floor. Compression *was* the deliverable — which is why the reasoning had to live somewhere the compression could not reach.

<FieldLog timestamp="Engagement 001 — execution audit" source="Field observation">
Council committed $5.6M. Twelve days later the executing agency had budgeted $0 against it. The commitment survived the handoff intact — the number was never in dispute. What did not survive was the reasoning that made it actionable: which line it belonged against, on whose authority, by when. The gap was caught only because the analytic layer still held the source graph and could re-enter it.
</FieldLog>

Nobody in that sequence did anything wrong. A format that cannot hold the reasoning will always strip it to fit — a container issue, not a personnel one. Distribution is what makes it structural. A solo practitioner who compresses their own work at least knows what was removed; a team cannot, because Person B never had Person A's reasoning, only Person A's document, already one layer removed. By the time the decision-maker asks *why this number*, the answer requires reconstructing a chain no one in the room holds in full.

<Callout kind="classified" title="Operating principle">
Coherence Decay is not a failure of quality control. It is a structural property of any production system where the reasoning and the presentation are held by different people. The only architectural response is to not separate them.
</Callout>

And the cost is invisible to the people paying it. Those clinicians did not know what they were missing; neither does any executive reading a brief.<Sidenote>Deloitte's TrustID Workforce Index found in Q2 2026 that 49 percent of workers with AI available use unapproved tools instead — senior leaders 32 percent more often than staff. That reads as a trust problem. It is closer to a coherence problem: the tool arrived as a deployment while the reasoning stayed with whoever had it.</Sidenote> Detecting the loss means holding the source *and* the compressed artifact at once and diffing them, which requires that someone still holds the source graph. In most production systems no one does: the analysis was an intermediate artifact, consumed by the summary and discarded.

## The architectural response

Sarif's answer is not *write better summaries*. It is structural, in three parts.

**Zero-handoff production.** One operator holds the reasoning from intake to signed deliverable; whoever derived the conclusion writes the sentence that communicates it. This is the argument of [Praxis No. 01](/praxis/one-operator-one-intelligence-layer/), and Coherence Decay is why it is not merely a staffing preference.

**Trace as connective tissue.** Every claim is wired to its evidence in the Trace, and when the deliverable is compressed downstream — and it will be — the Trace travels with it. Compression can strip the reasoning from the prose without killing it, because it lives in the audit layer rather than the body copy.

**Epistemic Mode as decay detection.** A claim tagged grounded whose source chain breaks downstream is a *detectable* break, precisely because the edge was explicit to begin with — the mechanism [Praxis No. 05](/praxis/signal-without-noise/) describes, viewed from the other end.

The first prevents decay. The second and third make it *survivable*, because prevention only holds inside the engagement and every deliverable eventually leaves.

## What this is not

**Not an argument that summaries are bad.** Compression is often the entire job — Engagement 001's leaders' brief existed to compress. The argument is that it be recoverable, not avoided.

**Not a measurement of our own fidelity loss.** A signed deliverable is itself a compression, and we have not run a controlled re-narration audit on our own output. If we do, we will publish the number whatever it says.

**Not free.** Retaining the source graph and refusing to split analysis from presentation cap how much work one operator can carry. That ceiling is the cost of the property, and we would rather pay it than sell volume.

## The claim

Coherence Decay is not a risk to be managed. It is the default state of distributed knowledge work — happening now in every organization that separates analysis from presentation and hands reasoning across role boundaries.

Those clinicians lost 30 percent under the best conditions anyone has constructed, and no consulting engagement is better resourced than a trauma bay. The question is not whether it is happening, but whether the architecture of production makes it visible, measurable and — where it matters — reversible.

<Pullquote classification="OPERATING POSITION" attribution="Praxis No. 06">
A deliverable is a graph. Coherence Decay snaps the edges long before the nodes go bad. The response is not better quality control. It is an architecture that keeps the edges explicit, portable and auditable — so that when compression happens, what was lost can be named.
</Pullquote>
